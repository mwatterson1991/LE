# Sales-deck rules: the Linear–Perplexity standard

## Build the buying argument
| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with something the buyer recognizes: a frustrating workflow, missed opportunity, or relevant change. | Spend the opening slides on company history, mission statements, and office locations. |
| Write headlines that make a point. "Your team rebuilds the same report every Monday" gives the slide a job. | Use empty labels such as "Overview," "Our Solution," or "Benefits" when a specific claim would work. |
| Connect each feature to a practical consequence. Show what becomes faster, easier, safer, or possible. | Make the buyer translate a feature inventory into business value. |
| Show the current approach and the proposed approach concretely. Compare steps, effort, cost, or outcomes fairly. | Manufacture urgency or use a comparison table that conveniently gives you every checkmark. |
| Place evidence beside the claim it supports. Use a relevant customer result, demonstration, or sourced metric. Include context and timeframe. | Treat a logo wall, investor backing, or an unsupported percentage as proof of customer results. |
| Explain how adoption works. Show the first step, responsibilities, likely effort, and how success will be judged. | Make switching look effortless when the buyer knows it requires work. |
| Close with one specific next step. State the action, participants, and purpose. | End with only "Thank you" or "Any questions?" |

## Make it look expensive
| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Give each slide one main message and one dominant focal point. Split a slide when two ideas compete. | Give every sentence, screenshot, and statistic equal visual weight. |
| Use a small typography system. One strong family, two or three weights, consistent heading/body sizes. | Introduce a new font, size, or style to solve every layout problem. |
| Use generous, consistent margins and deliberate alignment. Let important content have room around it. | Fill empty space because it is available, or shrink text to fit more content. |
| Choose a restrained palette. Neutral backgrounds and one accent; use color to direct attention. | Confuse "premium" with black backgrounds, gold accents, gradients, or low-contrast gray text. |
| Make product visuals readable. Crop to the relevant action and add a short annotation where needed. | Drop an entire application screen into a slide and expect the buyer to find the important detail. |
| Use images that carry meaning: real work, real products, relevant environments, or a useful diagram. | Decorate weak content with stock handshakes, random icons, or abstract shapes. |
| Repeat a few strong layouts and vary the pace. Alternate concise statements, product demonstrations, and evidence. | Make every slide a new design experiment, or force every idea into the same three-card layout. |

## Before sending
- Read only the headlines. They should form a coherent argument.
- Check the actual viewing size. Important text and product details must remain readable.
- Match the format to delivery. Live slides can be sparse; a forwarded deck needs enough context to stand alone.
- Remove anything that does no work. Each slide should establish relevance, explain value, provide evidence, resolve a concern, or advance the decision.
- Measure sales outcomes. Track useful next steps, qualified opportunities, and wins.
